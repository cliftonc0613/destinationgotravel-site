I hear it all the time: "I could just book this myself on my phone." And you could. The sites make it look effortless. So let me answer the question honestly, because I'd rather you know exactly what you're paying for than assume it's just a booking fee.

## What Does a Booking Site Actually Do for You?

A booking site shows you a calendar, a price, and a confirmation number. That's the whole relationship. It doesn't know that the "ocean view" room you picked actually looks at a parking structure, or that the flight connection you booked leaves you 40 minutes to sprint across an unfamiliar airport. It also won't tell you whether an [all-inclusive resort](/blog/all-inclusive-resorts-worth-it) is actually worth the price tag, or whether you'd be happier on an [ocean cruise or a river cruise](/blog/ocean-vs-river-cruise).

I've had clients come to me after a DIY booking went sideways more than once. The pattern is almost always the same: the price looked great, and the details that mattered weren't visible until they'd already landed. Even [USA.gov's travel resources](https://www.usa.gov/travel) recommend double-checking what a low listed price actually includes before you pay.

## Why use a travel agent instead of booking online

The honest answer is that a good travel agent isn't selling you convenience. We're selling you judgment, and access you can't get on your own.

**We know which room actually has the view.** Hotels don't publish their best room categories on booking sites. I've stayed at or personally vetted most of the properties I recommend, from cliffside stays on a [Santorini and Paros itinerary](/blog/santorini-paros-itinerary) to the resorts I send [empty nesters](/blog/best-european-river-cruises-empty-nesters) to in Europe, and I know which rooms are worth the upgrade and which ones aren't.

**We catch the problems before you book.** A connection that's too tight, a "resort fee" buried in the fine print, a destination that's in the middle of its rainy season. These are things I flag before you pay, not after.

**We fix it when something goes wrong.** This is the big one. If your flight gets canceled at 2am in a country where you don't speak the language, you're not stuck untangling refund and rebooking rules on your own from the gate. You're calling me. This is also why I always talk clients through [travel insurance](/travel-insurance) before a trip, and how it works alongside protections like the ones [the CFPB outlines for travel purchases](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/), not after something goes wrong.

## Are travel agents worth it? The math on cost

This is the question that actually matters, and it's a fair one. For most trips I book, my fee is small relative to what I save clients through rates, upgrades, and perks they wouldn't have access to booking direct. Many cruise lines, resorts, and tour operators pay agents directly, which means there's often no added cost to you at all. That renewed demand for agents, even as booking sites have gotten easier to use, is a big part of why the [travel agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel_agency) never went away.

Where it gets real is on a complicated trip. A multi-country itinerary, a big family reunion trip, a honeymoon where every detail matters. Pricing that out yourself across a dozen browser tabs takes hours, and a single mistake (wrong visa requirement, missed transfer window) can cost far more than any fee.

## Do travel agents cost more than booking yourself?

Usually not. On many hotel and cruise bookings, the rate you'd pay through me is the same rate you'd find yourself, because I'm often working with the same published rates plus added perks (room upgrades, resort credit, early check-in) that aren't available if you book direct. The commission comes from the supplier, not an extra charge stacked on your bill.

## When Does Booking It Yourself Make Sense?

I'll be straight with you: not every trip needs an agent. A simple weekend getaway to a city you already know well, a quick domestic flight, a hotel you've stayed at before. If you're confident in the destination and the details are simple, book it yourself and save the conversation for a trip that's more complicated.

Where I'd push back on going it alone: anything with multiple moving parts, anywhere you've never been, or any trip where a mistake would be expensive to fix. That's exactly where the reasons to use a travel agent start to outweigh the extra five minutes it takes to have the conversation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why use a travel agent for a cruise specifically?

Cruises have more moving parts than most trips: cabin category, deck location, dining time, shore excursions, and gratuities all affect the price. I know which cabins actually deliver the view or the quiet you want, and I can often get perks like onboard credit that aren't available booking direct.

### What are the disadvantages of using a travel agent?

The honest one is timing. Working with an agent means a conversation, not an instant checkout, so it's a poor fit if you want to book a simple trip at midnight on impulse. For anything with more than a couple of moving parts, that extra few minutes is what prevents the expensive mistakes.

### Will I pay the same rate as everyone else if I use a travel agent?

Usually, yes, and often better. On most hotel and cruise bookings I'm working with the same published rates you'd find yourself, plus perks like room upgrades or early check-in that come from the supplier relationship, not a markup added to your bill.

### How do I get started working with you?

Tell me where you want to go and what matters most about the trip. From there I put together options, walk you through the tradeoffs, and handle the booking once you're ready. No pressure to commit on the first conversation.

## The bottom line

Booking sites are great at showing you options. They're not built to know you, flag what won't work for your trip, or answer the phone when your plans fall apart at midnight. That's the difference, and it's the reason clients keep coming back to me instead of a search bar.
