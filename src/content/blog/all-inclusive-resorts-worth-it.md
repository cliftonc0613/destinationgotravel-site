The question I get more than any other from clients considering an all-inclusive trip is some version of: *"Are they actually worth it?"*

The honest answer is: some of them absolutely are. And some of them really aren't.

Here's what I've learned after years of researching and recommending all-inclusive resorts to empty nesters and retirees who want a genuinely relaxing trip — not a food court experience near a beach.

## What separates a good all-inclusive from a mediocre one

At the low end of the market, all-inclusive resorts work by spreading cost across a large number of guests — which means large properties, buffet-heavy dining, and a lot of time waiting in lines. The food is fine. The pool is crowded. The "premium" liquor costs extra.

At the mid-to-upper end, the experience shifts considerably. Smaller properties. À la carte restaurants (included in the rate). Butler service at some tiers. Private beach sections. The difference in day-to-day comfort is significant.

For my clients — who are typically looking for a trip they'll actually remember — I almost always steer toward the upper tier.

## My current recommendations by destination

### Turks & Caicos — COMO Parrot Cay

This is about as close to perfect as a resort gets for a couple looking for quiet, beauty, and exceptional service. It's a private island, so the pace is exactly what you set it to be. The water is absurdly blue. The food is excellent. If privacy and calm are your top priorities, this belongs at the top of your list.

### Jamaica — Sandals Royal Plantation

Most people think of Sandals as a big, energetic party resort — and some of them are. Royal Plantation is the exception. It's a small, adults-only, butler-service property on the quiet north coast. 74 suites. No swim-up bars, no entertainment decks. Just the ocean, extraordinary service, and one of the better wine lists I've seen at an all-inclusive.

### Mexico (Riviera Maya) — Excellence Playa Mujeres

Excellence is reliably excellent (the name earns it). The Playa Mujeres location is particularly strong — quieter than Cancun proper, with a beautiful stretch of beach and a room product that holds up well. The Excellence Club tier is worth the upgrade if you want the private pool access and dedicated concierge.

### Mexico (Los Cabos) — Chileno Bay Resort & Residences, Auberge Resorts

Technically this is more boutique hotel than traditional all-inclusive, but for clients who want the Los Cabos experience without a massive property, it's worth knowing about. The snorkeling at Chileno Bay is some of the best in Baja.

## What I tell clients to watch for

**Room categories matter more than the resort name.** At most properties, there's a meaningful difference between a standard room and a swim-up suite or butler villa. Before we book, I always look at which room tier actually delivers the experience you're expecting.

**Dining reservations fill up fast.** At the better resorts, the à la carte restaurants are legitimately good — but popular. Book your dinner reservations on the first day (or before you arrive, if the resort allows it). I handle this for my clients as part of the trip planning.

**Not all "premium" liquor is premium.** At most all-inclusives, even at the mid-to-upper tier, the included spirits are decent but not exceptional. If that matters to you, ask specifically about what's included at the bar before you book.

**Travel insurance is non-negotiable.** Weather, illness, schedule changes — any of these can affect an all-inclusive trip significantly. A good policy protects the investment.

## What to skip

I won't name individual resorts, but I'll give you the pattern: if a resort is advertising a very low nightly rate for an all-inclusive in a prime beach location, that's usually because they're making it up somewhere else — in the quality of food, the size of the pool, or the service ratios. I've seen clients come back from those trips disappointed. I'd rather find you something genuinely good.
