When my clients picture a river cruise, they usually imagine something like the brochure photos — champagne on the top deck, medieval villages sliding past the window, dinner that actually tastes good. And honestly? That's not far from reality. But there are a few things I always make sure to cover before anyone books.

## Not all river cruise lines are the same

The three lines I recommend most often to my clients are Viking, AmaWaterways, and Avalon Waterways. They each have a distinct personality:

- **Viking** is classic, slightly reserved, and exceptionally well-organized. The ships are beautiful and the included excursions are genuinely good.
- **AmaWaterways** leans toward active travelers — there are bikes on every ship and they encourage you to use them. If you love walking tours but want the option to cycle to the next village, AMA is worth a serious look.
- **Avalon** has a unique "open-air stateroom" concept — floor-to-ceiling windows that open wide. It's one of those things that sounds like marketing and turns out to actually matter.

I've researched all three extensively, and my recommendation always depends on you — not on which line has the better commission.

## The Rhine vs. the Danube

Most first-time river cruisers ask me: *Rhine or Danube?*

The **Rhine** (Amsterdam to Basel, roughly) is more compact, more dramatically scenic, and packed with medieval castles. It's a shorter cruise — usually 7 nights — and it's ideal if you want a lot of visual wow without a lot of transit time.

The **Danube** (Budapest to Nuremberg or Vienna) covers more ground, more cultures, and more variety. You'll see the Alps from the water, spend time in Budapest — which is genuinely one of the most beautiful cities in Europe — and pass through small towns that have barely changed in 200 years.

My honest take: if this is your first river cruise, start with the Rhine. If you've been to Europe before and want something richer and more varied, the Danube.

## When to go

River cruise season runs roughly April through October. My personal preference for most clients is **May** or **September**.

May means tulips (if you're doing the Rhine/Netherlands portion), mild weather, and thinner crowds. September means harvest season in the wine regions — the Moselle and Rhine valleys in particular are gorgeous — and the temperatures have cooled from the summer peak.

July and August are perfectly fine, but popular ports like Amsterdam and Prague can feel crowded, and the heat on the river in southern Europe can be intense.

## What's actually included (and what isn't)

Most premium river cruises include:
- All meals onboard
- Wine, beer, and soft drinks with lunch and dinner
- Daily excursions at every port
- Gratuities (on some lines)
- Port charges

What's usually *not* included:
- Specialty dining experiences onboard
- Shore excursion upgrades (bike tours, cooking classes, etc.)
- Airfare and pre/post cruise hotels
- Travel insurance

Speaking of insurance — don't skip it. River levels can force itinerary changes (high water means buses instead of boats; low water means the same). A good travel insurance policy means you're covered either way. I always build this into the trip.

## The part nobody tells you

The cabins are small. Not "cruise ship tiny" — more like "boutique hotel efficient." You're not spending much time in them, and the public spaces are beautifully designed, so it works out. But if you're used to the sprawling staterooms on ocean ships, it's worth knowing upfront.

Also: the pace is different from ocean cruising. You dock in a new town almost every day. That's the point — but it also means you're out and walking more than you might expect. Most of my clients love it. A few find it busier than they expected. I try to match people to the style of cruise that fits how they actually like to travel.
